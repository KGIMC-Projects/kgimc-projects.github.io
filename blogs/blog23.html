CHRISTIAN TEACHING • INNER HEALING & SPIRITUAL GROWTH

When God Heals the Root and Not Just the Symptoms

By Anita Singh

Sometimes we ask God to change our behavior, remove our struggles, or deliver us from patterns that keep repeating in our lives. We pray about anger, fear, rejection, insecurity, bitterness, and broken relationships. Yet even after experiencing temporary relief, we may find ourselves facing the same struggles again.

Why? Because sometimes we are dealing with the symptoms while the root remains untouched.

A person may struggle with anger, but underneath that anger may be years of rejection, betrayal, or unresolved pain. Someone may constantly seek approval because deep within they believe they are not worthy of love. Another person may struggle to trust others because past experiences have taught them to expect disappointment.

Not every struggle has a hidden emotional root, and we should never assume we know the cause of another person's difficulties. But there are times when God wants to bring healing beyond what is visible on the surface.

God is not only concerned with changing what we do. He is also concerned with transforming what is happening within our hearts.

The Difference Between the Root and the Fruit

Jesus teaches in Luke 6:43–45 that a tree is known by its fruit and that what comes out of a person's mouth reflects what is stored within the heart.

Luke 6:45 (NKJV) says, "For out of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaks."

Our words, reactions, attitudes, and choices can reveal what is taking place inside us. If bitterness is growing in the heart, it may eventually appear in our speech and relationships. If fear dominates our thinking, it may influence our decisions and prevent us from developing healthy trust.

Learning to manage our reactions is an important part of healing. Someone struggling with anger may need to practice pausing before responding, even while working through the experiences contributing to that anger. But when we focus only on controlling outward behavior without examining deeper beliefs, habits, or wounds, we may continue repeating the same patterns.

Healthy transformation often involves both practical changes in our behavior and deeper healing within our hearts.

The Bitter Waters of Marah: God Can Bring Change to What Is Bitter

In Exodus 15:22–25, after crossing the Red Sea, the Israelites traveled through the wilderness and came to Marah. They found water, but it was too bitter to drink. The people complained, and Moses cried out to the Lord.

God showed Moses a tree, which he cast into the water, and the water became drinkable.

This passage describes God's provision for Israel in the wilderness. Although it is not specifically a teaching about emotional healing, it gives us a meaningful illustration.

At Marah, God did not tell the Israelites to pretend the water was not bitter. He provided a way for the situation to change. In a similar way, genuine healing does not require us to deny what happened or pretend our pain does not exist.

We can bring the truth of our struggles before God and trust Him to guide us toward restoration.

God can meet us in the bitter places of life and lead us toward something different.

The Woman at the Well: Jesus Went Beyond the Surface

In John 4:4–26, Jesus encountered a Samaritan woman who came to draw water. Their conversation began with a natural need, but Jesus directed her attention toward the living water He could provide.

As the conversation continued, Jesus revealed that He knew important details about her marital history. Rather than reducing her to her circumstances, He engaged her in a conversation about worship, truth, and the Messiah.

Scripture does not explain every reason behind her relationships, so we should not assume we know the emotional causes of her situation. However, the encounter illustrates how Jesus can address a person's deeper spiritual need rather than stopping at what is visible.

The woman eventually returned to her community and told others about Jesus (John 4:28–30).

Jesus saw more than her circumstances. He offered her something that could transform her life from within.

Peter: Jesus Restored More Than His Position

Peter boldly declared that he would remain faithful to Jesus, yet he denied knowing Him three times during Jesus' arrest and trial (Luke 22:54–62). After the third denial, Peter went out and wept bitterly.

Following His resurrection, Jesus met with Peter and asked him three times whether he loved Him (John 21:15–19). Jesus then entrusted Peter with the responsibility of caring for His sheep.

Jesus did not merely give Peter another assignment. He addressed his love, reaffirmed his responsibility, and called him to follow Him.

This encounter illustrates restoration that goes beyond returning someone to a position. It involves renewing their relationship with Christ and calling them to faithful obedience.

God's restoration is not simply about putting us back where we were. It is about shaping who we are becoming.

Naaman: When Healing Requires Humility and Obedience

In 2 Kings 5:1–14, Naaman, a respected Syrian military commander, suffered from leprosy. He came seeking healing but became angry when Elisha instructed him to wash seven times in the Jordan River.

Naaman expected healing to happen in a way that matched his expectations. But after his servants encouraged him to obey the prophet's instructions, he humbled himself, went to the Jordan, and was healed.

The passage does not teach that Naaman's pride caused his disease. His physical condition and his attitude were distinct matters. Nevertheless, his response shows how pride and preconceived expectations can become obstacles to receiving what God is offering.

Sometimes the deeper work God is doing involves confronting the attitudes that keep us resistant to His instruction.

The Root of Bitterness Can Affect More Than One Person

Hebrews 12:15 warns believers to be watchful so that a root of bitterness does not spring up, cause trouble, and defile many.

Bitterness can develop when pain is repeatedly rehearsed, resentment is nurtured, and forgiveness is resisted. What began as a personal wound may eventually influence how we treat our families, respond to church leaders, relate to friends, or interpret the intentions of others.

A person carrying unresolved resentment may become defensive, suspicious, or quick to react. Over time, these responses can damage relationships and create conflict that affects many people.

Forgiveness is an important act of obedience, but emotional healing may take time. You can sincerely forgive someone and still need to work through the grief, fear, or pain caused by their actions. This does not automatically mean your forgiveness was insincere.

Forgiveness does not mean pretending the offense never happened, abandoning accountability, or returning to an unsafe relationship. It means choosing not to cultivate revenge and resentment while allowing God to work in our hearts.

Forgiveness releases the desire for personal revenge; healing addresses the ways the experience has affected your heart and life.

Healing the Root Requires Renewing the Mind

Romans 12:2 teaches that transformation involves the renewing of our minds. Many recurring struggles are connected not only to painful experiences but also to beliefs we have developed about ourselves, other people, and God.

Someone who has experienced repeated rejection may begin believing, "Nobody will ever love me." Someone who has been betrayed may believe, "I can never trust anyone." Someone who has experienced failure may believe, "I will never amount to anything."

These beliefs can influence our decisions, relationships, and expectations. Even when our circumstances change, we may continue responding according to what we have come to believe.

Renewing the mind involves allowing God's truth to challenge destructive beliefs and learning to think in ways that reflect His Word. It also requires practicing healthier responses instead of continually repeating old patterns.

For example, rather than believing that rejection determines our worth, we can remember that our value is not established by another person's acceptance. Rather than assuming everyone will betray us, we can learn to exercise wisdom, observe character, and develop trust gradually.

Healing is not only about what happened to us. It can also involve changing what we have come to believe because of what happened.

Practical Steps Toward Healing the Root

1. Invite God to search your heart. Pray as David did in Psalm 139:23–24, asking God to reveal what needs His attention.

2. Look beyond the immediate reaction. When anger, fear, or defensiveness repeatedly appears, consider what may be contributing to that response (James 1:19).

3. Acknowledge your pain honestly. You do not have to deny rejection, betrayal, grief, or loss to walk in faith (Psalm 34:18).

4. Repent where repentance is necessary. Take responsibility for sinful attitudes and actions without accepting blame for wrongs committed against you (1 John 1:9).

5. Choose forgiveness while maintaining wisdom. Release resentment, but recognize that trust, reconciliation, and emotional healing may require additional time and appropriate boundaries (Ephesians 4:31–32).

6. Renew your mind with God's truth. Identify destructive beliefs and replace them with thinking shaped by Scripture (Romans 12:2).

7. Practice healthy responses. Learn to communicate honestly, regulate reactions, and establish boundaries while deeper healing continues (Proverbs 4:23).

8. Receive appropriate support and allow time for growth. Prayer, mature pastoral guidance, trustworthy relationships, and qualified professional counseling can all be valuable. Some changes happen quickly, while others require patience and ongoing care (Proverbs 11:14; Philippians 1:6).

Final Reflection: Let God Work Beneath the Surface

Sometimes we ask God to remove the anger, fear, insecurity, bitterness, or destructive patterns we see in our lives, but we may not recognize what is happening beneath the surface.

God invites us to bring our whole hearts before Him—not only the parts we are comfortable showing others.

Healing does not mean pretending the pain never happened. It means allowing God's truth, grace, and wisdom to help us face what has happened and move toward freedom.

Sometimes that process includes repentance. Sometimes it includes forgiveness, grieving, renewing our minds, establishing boundaries, or receiving help from others.

We must remember that not every struggle has a hidden emotional root, and not every healing journey looks the same. Some changes happen quickly, while others require time, patience, and ongoing support.

God is not only interested in changing what people see on the outside. He is concerned with the condition of our hearts and the transformation of our lives.

When we allow Him to work beneath the surface, we can begin to experience change that reaches beyond temporary relief.

Don't become so focused on removing the symptoms that you overlook the deeper work God may be inviting you to do.

A Question to Consider

Are you continually asking God to remove the symptoms of a struggle while avoiding the deeper issue He may be inviting you to address?

Prayer

Heavenly Father, thank You that You see beyond what others can see and understand the deepest places of our hearts. Search us and reveal any bitterness, fear, insecurity, unhealthy beliefs, or unresolved pain that may be influencing our lives. Give us the courage to face what needs healing and the humility to surrender what needs to change. Help us forgive where forgiveness is needed, repent where repentance is necessary, and receive the wisdom and support that will help us grow. Renew our minds with Your truth, soften the places that have become hardened, and teach us to walk in freedom, love, and spiritual maturity. May the transformation You begin within us produce good fruit in every area of our lives. In Jesus' name, Amen.

— Anita Singh
